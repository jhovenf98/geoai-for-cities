Lithium Without the Dust: A GeoAI Plan for the Salton Sea

California wants the Salton Sea to supply lithium for electric vehicles. The people who live nearby already breathe some of the worst air in the state. This plan uses satellites, drones and public health data to measure what lithium extraction could add to that burden, and to test ways to prevent it.

By Jhoven (Johan) Fernandez

1.Why now

In August 2026, a California appeals court ruled that Imperial County unlawfully approved the largest proposed lithium project, Controlled Thermal Resources’ Hell’s Kitchen. The environmental review had not shown that the project could secure water for 50 years, and it had not addressed how the project’s water use could affect air quality near the Salton Sea. The review went back to the county.

That missing link is the center of this plan:

Each step can be measured from the air and from public data. Linking them is a spatial modeling problem, which makes it a natural GeoAI case study.

2.Where things stand

  • Hell’s Kitchen (Controlled Thermal Resources): planned up to 300,000 metric tons of lithium carbonate a year and about 50 MW of geothermal power. Its approval is back with the county after the August 2026 ruling.
  • CalEnergy (Berkshire Hathaway Energy): runs 10 geothermal plants at the Salton Sea, finished a lithium extraction pilot, and plans a demonstration plant in Calipatria by the end of 2026.
  • EnergySource Minerals (Project ATLiS): permitted, with no announced timeline.
  • The resource: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimates the brine already pumped each year carries about 127,000 metric tons of lithium carbonate equivalent, and the well-studied reservoir holds about 4.1 million tons (up to about 18 million under broader assumptions).
  • What residents and advocates are asking for: independent monitoring of water and air, safe handling of solid waste containing lead and arsenic, protection of the Southeast Lake Cahuilla Active Volcanic Cultural District, and a community fee paid to the towns closest to the projects.

3.How the lithium comes out, and where drones fit

The lithium is not dug up. It is dissolved in hot brine deep underground. Geothermal plants already pump that brine to the surface to make electricity; lithium projects add a step that pulls the lithium out of the brine (direct lithium extraction) before the brine is injected back underground.

Because the brine comes from deep wells and moves through closed pipes and plants, drones cannot do the extraction itself. What drones can do well is watch the surface around the operations: the shoreline, the lakebed, the plants, waste sites and the air, in far more detail than satellites. That makes them the independent monitoring layer residents are asking for.

4.Research design: three scales

SatellitesThe 25-year trendLandsat and Sentinel images map how much lakebed has been exposed since 2000, season by season. Radar (Sentinel-1) shows whether the ground is sinking near the well fields.
DronesThe detail on the groundCentimeter-scale maps of sample sites: which lakebed surfaces are crusted and which are loose and dusty, thermal views of plants and ponds, and gas readings downwind.
Monitors and peopleWho is exposedAir monitors and wind data link dust events to towns like Niland, Calipatria, Bombay Beach and Westmorland, and CalEnviroScreen health data shows who is already burdened.

The GeoAI part connects the scales. Drone maps label lakebed surfaces in detail at a few sites, a model learns those labels and applies them to satellite images across the whole shoreline, and a dust model translates exposed, loose surfaces and wind into exposure in each town. The test from Part 3 applies here too: a model trained on one stretch of shore has to prove it works on stretches it has not seen.

    What the dust monitors show

    Eight public PM10 monitors around the sea report to the EPA. From 2023 through 2025, at least one of them recorded a day above the federal 24-hour standard on 71 different days. Westmorland (30 days) and Brawley (29) had the most; Niland, the monitor closest to the geothermal field, had 19 and recorded the single highest reading on the Imperial side in 2025 (537 µg/m³, more than three times the standard).

    Bad days cluster in spring (April–May) and again in September–October. A monitor cannot tell where the dust came from: farm fields, open desert, construction or exposed lakebed. Separating those sources is the core job of the drone and satellite work in this plan, and it is the question the appeals court said the Hell’s Kitchen review never answered.

    Why PM2.5 looks low here: lakebed dust is mostly coarser particles (PM10), and CalEnviroScreen 4.0 does not include PM10. A low PM2.5 rank near the sea does not mean the dust problem is small. The PM10 monitors above tell a different story, but there are only eight of them for hundreds of square miles, and none of the eight sits on the exposed shoreline itself. Shoreline monitors run by other agencies, such as the Imperial Irrigation District, are not in the EPA files and still need to be checked for access.

    Data: CalEnviroScreen 4.0 (OEHHA, 2021); asthma is age-adjusted emergency room visits per 10,000 people, poverty is the share living below twice the federal poverty line, and indicator years run roughly 2015 to 2019. Tract shapes are simplified. The geothermal field outline is approximate and for orientation only, not a legal or resource boundary. Town points are approximate.     6.Case studies: what has worked, and the drawbacks

    Each of these places has tried part of what this plan proposes. None is a perfect match for the Salton Sea, so each card ends with what carries over. Filter by topic.

    • Dust control

      Owens Lake, California

      Dust control on a dried lake, 2001–today

      What happenedLos Angeles drained Owens Lake for its aqueduct and the lakebed became the largest single source of dust pollution in the country. Under court and air-district orders, the city now controls 48.6 square miles of lakebed with shallow flooding, gravel, managed vegetation and other methods, and reports a 99.4% cut in dust emissions. Days above the federal standard at near-lake monitors fell from 49 in 2002 to 8 in 2018.

      DrawbacksMore than $2.5 billion in costs to ratepayers and about 60,000 acre-feet of water a year. Since 2007, dust control has used about 31% of the city’s fresh water supply on average. A National Academies review found no method yet proven to control dust while using much less water, and some methods disturb cultural sites.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaDust control works but is expensive and thirsty. The Salton Sea has no spare water, so mapping which patches of lakebed actually reach people, and controlling those first, matters more here.

      Source: LADWP: Owens Lake · National Academies (2020)

    • Drones

      Drones at Owens Lake

      Air district uses drones to check dust controls

      What happenedThe Great Basin air district flies drones 1.5 to 3 feet over treated lakebed so the rotor wash tests whether the surface gives off dust, logging particle levels, GPS position and height. It also uses drones for elevation models of surface roughness and for vegetation mapping.

      DrawbacksHobby-grade drones broke too often and were replaced with professional models. Flight height has to be recalibrated for each payload. The only account I found is a drone maker’s case study, with no published accuracy results.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaThe closest real precedent for this plan’s drone missions. It works for checking surfaces, but it needs professional equipment, careful calibration, and independent validation before results go in a report.

      Source: Draganfly case study (vendor)

    • Dust control

      Salton Sea dust sources

      UC Riverside chemical fingerprinting, 2015–16

      What happenedResearchers matched the chemistry of airborne particles at Salton City and Bombay Beach to their sources. Lakebed-like (playa) dust made up about 9% of PM10 on average (about 12% in summer, 5% in winter); crustal dust, which includes desert soil, was about 45%. They expected the lakebed share to grow as the sea shrinks.

      DrawbacksSamples cover about two and a half weeks in August 2015 and February 2016 at two sites, and no dust storms, which is when lakebed dust matters most.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaThis is why the dust monitors on the map cannot settle the question alone. Storm-day sampling at several sites, plus drone surface maps, is needed to separate lakebed dust from desert and farm dust.

      Source: Frie et al. (2017), Environmental Science & Technology

    • Dust control

      Salton Sea Management Program

      State habitat and dust projects, target 2028

      What happenedCalifornia committed to cover 29,800 acres of exposed lakebed with habitat or dust suppression by 2028. The state’s progress dashboard lists 4,782 acres done, about 16% of the goal, including 2,425 acres of dust suppression.

      DrawbacksProgress is far behind the target with two years left, and the dashboard’s own totals do not fully agree with each other. Mitigation that is promised is not mitigation that exists.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaScenario 3 (full build-out with mitigation) should model mitigation arriving late or partly, not on schedule.

      Source: Salton Sea Management Program dashboard

    • Lithium & geothermal

      Salton Sea geothermal field and earthquakes

      Science, 2013

      What happenedComparing earthquake records from 1981 to 2012 with plant operating data, researchers found earthquake rates at the Salton Sea geothermal field track the volume of fluid pumped out and injected. Most induced quakes were small; the largest in the period was magnitude 5.1.

      DrawbacksThe field sits at the southern end of the San Andreas fault. The authors called a link to a large quake plausible but not established. Lithium projects would add brine flow at the same field.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaGround and seismic monitoring belong in the plan, not just dust. Radar ground-motion maps (already approach 3 in the Methods table) should be read alongside public earthquake catalogs as pumping changes.

      Source: Brodsky & Lajoie (2013), Science

    • Lithium & geothermal

      Upper Rhine Valley, Germany

      Geothermal lithium, the closest overseas match

      What happenedVulcan Energy’s Lionheart project pairs geothermal power and heat (planned 275 GWh of power and 560 GWh of heat a year) with lithium extraction from the same hot brine, aiming for 24,000 tonnes of lithium hydroxide a year. Construction began in December 2025, and its €2.2 billion financing closed in May 2026.

      DrawbacksLandau already has a history: in 2009 the geothermal plant there was followed by small quakes, the largest magnitude 2.7. A 2013 study found the shaking reached the damage guideline for very sensitive buildings; residents reported hairline cracks in plaster, though whether the quakes caused them was disputed. Lionheart has not yet produced lithium, so its environmental record is still untested.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaGeothermal lithium is moving ahead elsewhere, with monitoring and liability rules written after problems appeared. Imperial County can set those rules before projects start.

      Source: Vulcan ASX announcement (28 May 2026) · Groos et al. (2013)

    • Water

      Salar de Atacama, Chile

      Lithium from brine in a desert basin

      What happenedA 2022 study found that lithium mining uses less than 10% of the basin’s fresh water, but total water use appears to exceed what the basin takes in. More than half of the water feeding its wetlands is at least 60 years old, so damage from over-use can show up decades later.

      DrawbacksChile uses evaporation ponds, a different method from geothermal extraction, so the numbers do not transfer directly. Local and Indigenous communities have long disputed how water is shared.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaJudge a project by the whole basin’s water budget, not its own share alone, and plan monitoring over decades, not a permit cycle.

      Source: ScienceDaily on Moran et al., Earth’s Future (2022)

    • Money

      Smackover Formation, Arkansas

      How a state priced lithium from brine

      What happenedArkansas regulators set a 2.5% royalty on lithium produced from brine, tied to market prices, for projects by ExxonMobil and by Standard Lithium with Equinor in 2025. Standard Lithium and Equinor said they were aiming to produce lithium in 2028.

      DrawbacksRoyalties tied to price swing with the market, and lithium prices were low when they were approved. The royalty is set by production, not by who lives near the wells.

      Lesson for the Salton SeaA useful contrast for Scenario 4: payments can be designed around output or around exposure, and the choice decides which towns benefit.

      Source: Fastmarkets (2025)

    9.Where predictive modeling comes in

    The chain from water to dust to people is a set of predictions, one feeding the next. Each model below answers one link. Together they run every scenario in section 12, so a decision about water or mitigation can be judged by the dust and exposure it is likely to cause before anything is built. Tap a model.

    →→→→

    Model 1: How much lakebed will be exposed?

    Predicts
    Yearly sea level and exposed lakebed area under each scenario, through 2050.
    Inputs
    Inflow projections (farm drainage, Colorado River deliveries, project water use), evaporation, and the sea’s elevation-to-area curve.
    Method
    A water-balance model, not machine learning. The physics is known, so a simple model is easier to check and explain.
    How it is tested
    Hindcast: run it from 2000 and compare its predicted shoreline with the shoreline mapped from satellite images through 2025.
    Drawback
    Future inflows are policy choices, not physics. The model can only be as good as the water assumptions fed into it.

    Model 2: Which lakebed will give off dust?

    Predicts
    A map of surface type for every 10-meter pixel: hard crust, loose sediment, wet, or vegetated.
    Inputs
    Sentinel-2 and Landsat images, Sentinel-1 radar (roughness and moisture), elevation, time since exposure. Training labels come from drone orthomosaics of sample plots.
    Method
    A random forest baseline first, then a deep-learning image model (U-Net) if it clearly beats the baseline. This is the core GeoAI piece.
    How it is tested
    Spatial-block cross-validation: hold out whole stretches of shoreline the model never saw, and report accuracy for each surface class, not one overall score.
    Drawback
    Few labeled plots, surfaces that change after every rain, and newly exposed lakebed that may look unlike anything in the training data.

    Model 3: When will dust exceed the standard?

    Predicts
    Daily PM10 at each monitor and the chance of a day above the federal standard, including a 1 to 3 day warning from weather forecasts.
    Inputs
    Wind speed and direction, how much loose lakebed lies upwind, soil moisture, season, recent rain, and the surface map from Model 2. Targets are the EPA PM10 readings shown on the map.
    Method
    Gradient-boosted trees that output a probability, compared against two simple baselines: “same as yesterday” and the average for that month.
    How it is tested
    Train on earlier years and test on 2024–2025; leave one monitor out at a time to test places without a monitor. It only counts if it beats both baselines.
    Drawback
    Monitors cannot tell lakebed dust from farm or desert dust, there are only eight of them, and the worst dust days are rare, which makes them hard to predict.

    Model 4: Who breathes it, and how much more?

    Predicts
    Extra dust days per person in each census tract and town under each scenario, compared with no new lithium.
    Inputs
    Model 3’s predictions spread across a grid, population by tract, and the CalEnviroScreen health and poverty data on the map.
    Method
    Population-weighted exposure, then a statistical model of asthma ER visits against exposure that adjusts for poverty, age and access to care.
    How it is tested
    Check that predicted high-exposure tracts line up with where monitors and residents report dust. Report ranges, not single numbers.
    Drawback
    Tract data shows patterns across areas, not what happens to any one person, so health links here are associations, not proof of cause.

    Model 5: Is the ground or seismicity changing?

    Predicts
    Early warning when ground sinking or earthquake rates near the well fields move outside their normal range.
    Inputs
    Sentinel-1 radar ground-motion time series, public earthquake catalogs, and brine pumped and injected (the link found at this field in 2013).
    Method
    Anomaly detection on the radar time series, plus a model of earthquake rate against fluid volume.
    How it is tested
    Backtest on past years: would it have flagged known changes, and how often does it raise false alarms?
    Drawback
    Pumping data comes from operators and may be delayed or summarized. An alert says something changed, not why.

    How I will know a model works

    A model that scores well on random test points can still fail on a stretch of shore it has never seen, because nearby points look alike. That was the main lesson of Part 3. So every model is tested on places or years held out completely.

    Random split: test squares sit next to training squares, so the model is graded on places it has nearly seen. Scores look better than they will be in practice.

    Spatial blocks: a whole stretch of shoreline is held out. This is closer to the real question: will the model work on lakebed that has not been mapped by drone?

    Training Test

    • Beat a simple baseline. Every model is compared with the obvious guess (last year’s shoreline, yesterday’s dust, the monthly average). If it cannot beat that, it is not used.
    • Show uncertainty. Predictions come with ranges, and the scenario results report the spread across water assumptions, not one number.
    • Publish the errors. Accuracy by surface class, by monitor and by year goes in the report, including where the models do badly.
    Why this matters beyond research: a reliable 1 to 3 day dust forecast is useful on its own. Schools could move recess indoors, clinics could prepare, and the county could time dust control on the patches that matter. It also has to prove itself on past seasons before anyone relies on it, which is why the testing above comes first.

    10.Drone missions

    1. Shoreline and lakebed mapping. Repeat flights over fixed sample plots each season to map exposed lakebed and surface type (crust, loose sediment, vegetation) at centimeter scale. These become the training labels for the satellite model.
    2. Thermal surveys. Thermal cameras over brine ponds, pipelines and natural mudpots to spot leaks and changes in surface heat.
    3. Gas and particle sampling. Small sensors carried downwind of plants to check hydrogen sulfide and dust levels independently of company reports.
    4. Waste and ground change. 3D models of solid-waste piles and well pads to track volume and surface change over time.
    Rules to plan around: flights follow FAA Part 107 (licensed pilot, under 400 feet, in sight of the pilot, airspace authorization where needed). Launching or landing drones on national wildlife refuges, including Sonny Bono Salton Sea National Wildlife Refuge, is prohibited, and drones must not disturb birds. Plant sites need the owner’s permission, and anything near the cultural district needs the tribes’ consent first.

    12.Scenarios to compare

    Each scenario changes one thing and runs through the same chain from water to dust to people. No results are shown yet; these are the comparisons the model is built to make.

    No new lithium

    What changes
    Existing geothermal plants only. The sea keeps shrinking on its current path as inflows fall.
    Question
    Exposed lakebed and dust exposure from inflow trends alone. Every other scenario is measured against this one.
    What gets mapped
    Lakebed area by year, dust days per town, people exposed by burden class (map above).
    Biggest unknown
    How fast inflows fall, which depends on farm water use and transfers more than on lithium.

    One project, two water futures

    What changes
    Hell’s Kitchen runs at its planned scale, modeled with normal Colorado River deliveries and with cuts.
    Question
    How much the project’s own water use adds to lakebed exposure, and whether that grows when the river is cut.
    What gets mapped
    Extra exposed lakebed and extra dust days in Niland, Calipatria and Westmorland compared with the baseline.
    Biggest unknown
    The project’s final water source and volume, which the court said were not settled.

    All projects, with and without mitigation

    What changes
    Every proposed project is built. One version has no mitigation; the other adds dust control on exposed lakebed, habitat ponds and a community impact fee.
    Question
    Whether mitigation can offset the added exposure, and where it should go first.
    What gets mapped
    Exposure with and without mitigation, and a priority map of lakebed patches where dust control protects the most people.
    Biggest unknown
    Whether mitigation money actually arrives and keeps pace with the shoreline.

    Who gets paid, who bears the risk

    What changes
    The county’s current revenue split by city size, compared with a split weighted by modeled exposure.
    Question
    Whether the towns closest to the plants and dust receive a share that matches what they live with.
    What gets mapped
    Dollars per resident by town under each split, set against exposure from the scenarios above.
    Biggest unknown
    Actual revenue depends on lithium prices and output, both uncertain; the comparison is about shares, not totals.

    13.First post: “The Shrinking Shore”

    A first public piece can be done without drones or new fieldwork: map how much lakebed has been exposed since 2000 using free satellite images and the sea’s recorded water levels, then show which towns sit downwind of the newly exposed areas and how their asthma rates compare. It sets up every later step and stands on public data alone.

    14.Limits and responsibilities

    • Lithium resource estimates vary widely, and how fast the reservoir replenishes is not yet known.
    • Linking dust to a specific project is hard: the sea is shrinking for many reasons, mostly reduced water inflows. The question is how much a project adds, not whether it causes the problem.
    • This work should be done with the communities it studies. Local groups such as Comité Cívico del Valle already monitor air quality and have shaped the debate; tribal leaders decide what happens near cultural sites.
    • Several data sources, including community sensor networks, still need to be checked for access before they are relied on.
